# 21 · Dispensed medicine label

Sample `SYN-0016`, printed in small type at the bottom right of the page. One page, and that page is the front of a medicine carton rather than a sheet of paper. A pharmacy dispensing label from a fictional pharmacy, Ledworth Central Pharmacy, made out to Margaret Wilson and stuck across the front of the carton her tablets were dispensed in. The answer key is [`ground-truth.json`](ground-truth.json).

## Where it comes from

The white label followed an official sample. The national standard for labelling dispensed medicines prints an example label, Figure 2.1, and that example was followed all the way through: which blocks appear, in what order, and how big the type is in each one. The carton underneath had no sample to follow. No government body publishes a design for the front of a medicine box. So the carton was built up from rules alone. It was kept as plain as those rules allow.

- **National standard for labelling dispensed medicines** (Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care, 2021). This is what the whole label rests on. Section 3.1 gives the label size, 80 mm wide and 40 mm high, which is the size most Australian pharmacy dispensing systems print. Standard 1 gives the three type sizes, and it puts the pharmacy name, address and phone in the third and smallest of them. Standard 2 and Figure 2.1 give the order of the blocks down the label. Standard 3 requires the two signal words "Active ingredient:" and "Brand name:" on their own separate lines. Standard 5 says the dose form must not be shortened, which is why the label says "tablets" and not "tabs". Standard 6 says the dose is written in numbers. Standard 12 gives the machine readable code. Figure 2.1 is the only official picture of a dispensing label that was used. <https://www.safetyandquality.gov.au/sites/default/files/resources/attachments//labelling2-publish-web-072aug21.pdf>
- **Therapeutic Goods (Poisons Standard June 2026) Instrument 2026** (Commonwealth, Federal Register of Legislation). Appendix L clause 1 is the list of what a dispensing label must carry: the name, address and phone of the pharmacy that dispensed it, the generic name and the brand name, adequate directions for use, the strength and the dose form, the total quantity supplied, the words "Keep out of reach of children" in red on white, who the medicine was dispensed for, the prescription reference number, and the date it was dispensed. Every line on the white label is there because this clause asks for it. The label does not quote it. <https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2026L00633/asmade/2026-05-28/text/original/pdf>
- **Therapeutic Goods Order No. 91** (Commonwealth, Federal Register of Legislation). It says the label on a medicine container must leave a blank space of at least 70 mm by 30 mm for the dispensing label. That is why the carton front has a clear area below the coloured band, and the 80 mm by 40 mm label sits inside it with room to spare. The carton does not quote it. <https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2016L01285/2024-12-07/2024-12-07/text/original/pdf>
- **Drugs, Poisons and Controlled Substances Regulations 2017** (Victoria). Regulation 72 adds the Victorian requirements for a prescription medicine container: the date the supply record was made, and the directions for use. Both are on this label. <https://www.legislation.vic.gov.au/in-force/statutory-rules/drugs-poisons-and-controlled-substances-regulations-2017>
- **Packaging and Labelling of Medicines** (Pharmacy Guild of Australia). It says a pharmacy label must not say "use as directed" or anything equivalent. That is why the directions here are specific and say when to take the tablet. <https://www.guild.org.au/>
- **Guidelines on dose administration aids and staged supply of dispensed medicines** (Pharmacy Board of Australia). Used the other way round. It was read to confirm that this sample is a single medicine label and not a dose administration aid, because a dose administration aid has its own list of required content and its own physical shape. <https://www.pharmacyboard.gov.au/>
- **Frequently asked questions** (Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme). It says that since 1 August 2003 the full cost of the medicine has had to be listed on the labelling. That is why the label carries the "Full cost of medicine" line. <https://www.pbs.gov.au/general/faq>
- **Fees, Patient Contributions and Safety Net Thresholds** (Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme). It gives the \$7.70 that a concession card holder pays for a subsidised medicine, which is the figure printed on the label. <https://www.pbs.gov.au/info/healthpro/explanatory-notes/front/fee>

Made up, not taken from any source: the pharmacy Ledworth Central Pharmacy, its address at 12 Ashmead St, Calderfield VIC 3999 and its phone number, the patient Margaret Wilson, the prescribing doctor Dr Susan Patel, the prescription reference number, the dispenser's initials in "Disp/Ck: R.T.", and the bar code. The expiry date 09/2028 sits inside the one to three years after dispensing that the spec card gives, and the exact month was chosen. The full cost of \$19.42 was also chosen. The real price of this medicine is published in the scheme's own schedule and it was not looked up for this label. The only requirement on that figure is that it is larger than the \$7.70 a concession card holder pays. The shape of the reference number, "Ref #" then digits then three letters, is one of the two shapes the official examples use, and no rule for how a pharmacy makes these numbers up was found. The "Brand name:" line repeats the active ingredient because the medicine is an unbranded generic, where the product name is the ingredient name. That choice was made because printing a real brand name is not allowed, and inventing a brand name is not allowed either. The carton is almost all invention. Its size of 112 mm by 66 mm has no source, because the rules say how much space a carton must leave for the label and say nothing about how big the carton is. Its plain coloured band with no artwork is a deliberate choice, because a synthetic carton must not look like any real manufacturer's pack. The carton also leaves off the sponsor company name and the medicine's registration number, which a real carton carries, because one would need a real company name and the other a number from a real national register. The poisons information centre number is left off too, because it is advice rather than a requirement and printing it would need a real phone number.

## The six fields

### document_type

**Dispensed medicine label.** Page 1, the white label lying across the carton. Its second and third lines read "Active ingredient: Perindopril arginine 5 mg tablets" and "Brand name: Perindopril arginine". The red line above them says "Keep out of reach of children", and the smallest line at the foot of the label names the pharmacy that dispensed the medicine. The answer key stores it as `dispensed_medicine_label`. This field is free text and the experiments do not score it.

### issuer

**Ledworth Central Pharmacy.** Page 1, the bottom line of the white label, in the smallest type on the page: "Ledworth Central Pharmacy, 12 Ashmead St, Calderfield VIC 3999, Ph: (03) 5550 9722". The name appears nowhere else on the page. It is the only organisation named anywhere on the label or the carton, and it is the pharmacy that dispensed the medicine and printed the label.

### action_required

**Take medicine perindopril each morning.** Page 1, the middle of the white label, the largest instruction on it: "Take 1 tablet in the morning". The line under it says "Take at least half an hour before food". The medicine is named at the top of the label, "Active ingredient: Perindopril arginine 5 mg tablets", and again in the large type on the carton band above the label. The value starts with one of the contract's eight action words, and the experiments score that word.

### due_date

**Not applicable.** No date to act by is printed on the label. The label carries two dates. "08/07/26" sits at the top of the right column, and it is the date the medicine was dispensed. "Expiry date: 09/2028" sits in the left column under the quantity, and it is the date the tablets stop being good. Neither one is a date by which Margaret has to do something. The instruction "Take 1 tablet in the morning" is a standing daily instruction with no end date printed beside it. The answer key has `null` for this field.

### amount

**No payment required.** Page 1, the white label asks for no money anywhere. There is no payment method, no pay by date, no balance, and no tear off slip. Two money figures are printed. "\$7.70" is in bold at the foot of the right column, beside the dispensing details, and "Full cost of medicine \$19.42" is in the left column, which is the line the medicines scheme requires a label to carry. The answer key has `null` for this field.

### reference

**6429746 DFD.** Page 1, the right column of the white label, third line, "Ref #6429746 DFD". The words printed beside the value are "Ref #", and the value itself is seven digits, a space, then three letters. A bar code is printed at the bottom right of the label, with no digits under it. The value is printed only once, in the right column. The contract asks for the reference, account or customer number the person must quote, and the prescription reference is the number this supply is recorded under at the pharmacy.
